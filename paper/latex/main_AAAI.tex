\begin{abstract}
%Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problems with Time Windows (DVRPTW) require sequential policies to simultaneously coordinate fleet-wide decisions and anticipate long-term routing consequences under uncertainty. Existing shared policies often entangle these two aspects within a single scoring mechanism, leading to suboptimal decisions and limited generalization. Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problems with Time Windows (DVRPTW) require sequential routing policies to make decisions that are simultaneously feasible in the short term, coordinated across multiple vehicles, and effective over long planning horizons under dynamic customer arrivals. Existing neural routing policies typically compress these heterogeneous objectives into a single compatibility score, making coordination and anticipation difficult to learn jointly. We propose COAST (Coordinated Ownership and Anticipatory Sequential Routing), a neural routing framework that reformulates customer selection as a decision decomposition problem. Specifically, COAST estimates three complementary decision signals: Edge-aware Compatibility for immediate routing feasibility, Ownership Memory for implicit fleet coordination, and Candidate-conditioned Lookahead for anticipatory decision making. These signals are adaptively fused to produce the final routing policy and are optimized end-to-end through reinforcement learning without auxiliary supervision. Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent improvements over classical and neural baselines, together with strong cross-scale transfer and stable performance across controlled dynamic operating conditions. Ablation studies confirm that the three decision signals provide complementary contributions. These results suggest that explicitly decomposing routing decisions into complementary reasoning components provides an effective framework for dynamic vehicle routing.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) is one of the most extensively studied combinatorial optimization problems, with broad applications in logistics, transportation, and last-mile delivery. Classical VRP formulations assume that all customer requests and travel information are available before optimization begins \cite{toth2014vehicle}. However, many real-world logistics systems operate in highly dynamic environments, where customer requests arrive continuously during execution, traffic conditions evolve over time, and routing decisions must be updated online. These practical requirements give rise to the Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problem (DVRP), which more accurately models applications such as on-demand delivery, ride-sharing, and urban logistics \cite{psaraftis1988dynamic}.

Among its variants, the Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (DVRPTW) is particularly challenging because each request is associated with temporal and capacity constraints while future customer arrivals remain unknown. Unlike static routing, where globally optimized routes can be computed offline, a routing policy must repeatedly select the next customer for each vehicle under incomplete information. Decisions that appear locally optimal may adversely affect future routing opportunities, making effective online planning essential.

Traditional approaches to DVRPTW primarily rely on dispatching rules, metaheuristics with periodic re-optimization, or rolling-horizon optimization frameworks. Although these methods have demonstrated competitive performance in many settings, they often require extensive problem-specific engineering and repeated optimization during execution, making them difficult to generalize across different dynamic environments. Recently, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising alternative by learning routing policies directly from data, enabling real-time decision making without solving a new optimization problem whenever the environment changes.

Despite this progress, learning-based routing policies still struggle to simultaneously coordinate multiple vehicles and anticipate the long-term consequences of online decisions. Existing sequential routing policies typically employ a shared neural policy to make decisions for all vehicles. While this formulation naturally enables parameter sharing and implicit coordination, it also requires the policy to balance fleet-level coordination and long-term planning within a single decision process. As a result, the learned policy may become less robust when customer arrival patterns, spatial distributions, or time-window characteristics differ from those observed during training.

In this paper, we argue that coordination and long-term planning represent two fundamentally different reasoning processes and should be modeled explicitly rather than implicitly. Motivated by this observation, we propose COAST, a reinforcement learning framework that separates customer selection into complementary decision signals. COAST combines an edge-aware compatibility module for modeling local vehicle--customer interactions, a memory-based ownership mechanism for implicit fleet coordination, and a candidate-conditioned lookahead signal that differentiates actions through long-horizon policy feedback. These complementary signals are adaptively fused into a unified routing policy that is trained end-to-end using reinforcement learning.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We present a structured decision decomposition framework for DVRPTW that explicitly separates fleet coordination from long-term planning within sequential routing policies.
    \item We introduce a memory-based ownership mechanism that enables implicit vehicle coordination without explicit inter-vehicle communication.
    \item We introduce a candidate-conditioned anticipatory scoring head that provides action-specific evidence optimized through long-horizon policy returns.
    \item Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent improvements over classical and neural baselines, together with strong cross-scale transfer and stable performance across controlled dynamic operating conditions.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the proposed COAST framework. Section~\ref{sec:experiment} reports experimental results, and Section~\ref{sec:futurework} concludes the paper.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a competitive paradigm for neural combinatorial optimization in VRPs \cite{nazari2018reinforcement}. Early work used recurrent encoder--decoder policies to learn routing heuristics directly from data \cite{nazari2018reinforcement}, while attention-based Transformers substantially improved representation learning and solution quality across VRP variants \cite{kool2018attention}. More recent studies enhance scalability, generalization, and expressive power through transferable local policies \cite{gao2023towards}, heterogeneous attention \cite{wang2024deep}, lightweight cross-attention \cite{luo2025boosting}, edge-aware Transformers \cite{meng2025eformer}, and diverse decoding strategies \cite{hottung2025polynet}. MVMoE and RouteFinder further target multi-task generalization across static VRP variants \cite{zhou2024mvmoe,berto2025routefinder}, while RL4CO supports standardized and reproducible evaluation of neural combinatorial optimization methods \cite{berto2025rl4co}. Despite these advances in representation, search, diversity, and transfer, routing decisions are typically generated by a single vehicle--customer compatibility function that entangles local feasibility, fleet coordination, and long-term routing considerations.

For DVRP, reinforcement learning is an attractive alternative to repeatedly solving an optimization model online. Existing studies formulate the problem as a Markov decision process and learn dispatching policies with value-based or actor--critic reinforcement learning \cite{akkerman2025comparison,konovalenko2024optimizing}. Recent work incorporates evolving request information into dynamic policies \cite{konovalenko2024optimizing,bono2021mardam}, while graph and edge-aware encoders improve structured routing representations \cite{meng2025eformer}. Although these advances improve policy quality and generalization, decisions remain primarily driven by a unified compatibility score, making it difficult to distinguish immediate feasibility from the long-term consequence of selecting each customer.

Another fundamental challenge is coordinating multiple vehicles in dynamic environments. Multi-agent routing policies address this through event-driven sequential decisions, shared attention, or learned coordination mechanisms \cite{bono2021mardam,zhang2020multi,shahbazian2025knowledge}. While such strategies improve cooperation, they generally require repeatedly modelling the fleet during decision making, increasing computational cost and limiting scalability. Furthermore, standard actor--critic methods estimate a state-value function for variance reduction. Because it is independent of the selected action, the critic provides identical guidance to all candidate customers in a state and cannot explicitly assess their different downstream routing consequences.

Taken together, existing learning-based routing policies exhibit three important limitations: (i) local feasibility, fleet coordination, and long-term planning are entangled within a single compatibility score; (ii) effective vehicle coordination often relies on computationally expensive fleet-level interaction; and (iii) state-value critics provide action-independent guidance that cannot explicitly assess the long-term impact of individual routing decisions. To address these limitations, we propose COAST, which decomposes customer selection into three complementary signals: an edge-aware compatibility module for local feasibility, a memory-based ownership mechanism for implicit fleet coordination, and a candidate-conditioned lookahead signal for action-specific anticipatory evidence. These signals are adaptively fused into a unified routing policy trained end-to-end using reinforcement learning.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:prob}

\paragraph{Dynamic DVRPTW.}
A problem instance contains depot $0$ and customer requests $\mathcal C=\{1,\ldots,L_c\}$. Each request $j$ has normalized position $\mathbf p_j$, demand $q_j$, time window $[e_j,l_j]$, service duration $\sigma_j$, and reveal time $\rho_j$:
\[
\mathbf x_j=(\mathbf p_j,q_j,e_j,l_j,\sigma_j,\rho_j).
\]
At decision event $t$, the active vehicle $k_t$ is available at physical time $\eta_t=t^{\mathrm{cur}}_{k_t}$. Thus the policy observes only the revealed and unserved requests
\[
\mathcal C_t=\{j\in\mathcal C:\rho_j\leq\eta_t\},\qquad
\mathcal U_t=\mathcal C_t\setminus\mathcal S_t,
\]
where $\mathcal S_t$ is the served set. Vehicle $k$ has state $\mathbf s_k^t=(\mathbf p_k^t,q_k^{\mathrm{rem}},t_k^{\mathrm{cur}})$, and $s_t$ denotes the policy-visible event state comprising the fleet, revealed-request status, and active vehicle. For candidate $j$, let
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_{k_tj}&=\|\mathbf p_{k_t}^t-\mathbf p_j\|_2,\\
b_{k_tj}&=\max\!\left(t_{k_t}^{\mathrm{cur}}+d_{k_tj}/v,e_j\right),\\
\ell_{k_tj}&=[b_{k_tj}-l_j]_+ .
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $b_{k_tj}$ is the service-start time, including any wait before $e_j$. We use soft time windows: late service is penalized, rather than masked, as in multi-vehicle soft-window routing \cite{zhang2020multi, bono2021mardam}.

\paragraph{Event-driven sequential process.}
Following the event-driven sequential Multi-agent Markov Decision Process (sMMDP) construction of \citet{bono2021mardam}, only one homogeneous vehicle acts at each event. With $\mathcal D_t$ denoting completed vehicles,
\[
\begin{aligned}
k_t&=\arg\min_{k\notin\mathcal D_t}t_k^{\mathrm{cur}},\\
\mathcal A_t&\equiv\mathcal A(s_t,k_t)=\{0\}\cup
\{j\in\mathcal U_t:q_j\leq q_{k_t}^{\mathrm{rem}}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The depot is always eligible. The mask excludes unrevealed, served, capacity-ineligible, and completed-vehicle actions; it does not exclude late customers. After $a_t$, the environment updates travel, waiting, service, capacity, customer status, newly visible requests, and the next earliest-available vehicle. If the depot is selected while requests remain hidden, the simulator advances to the next reveal event instead of terminating.

\paragraph{Objective.}
The shared policy $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t,k_t)$ minimizes a shaped cost comprising travel distance, tardiness, and pending requests:
\[
J(\pi_\theta)=\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[
\sum_t\!\left(d_{k_t,a_t}+\lambda_{\mathrm{late}}\ell_{k_t,a_t}
+\zeta_t\lambda_{\mathrm{pend}}N_t\right)\right].
\]
where $\zeta_t$ indicates that the base routing transition marks the fleet complete and $N_t$ is its number of unserved requests. This wording avoids treating the penalty as terminal-only when a hidden request can reactivate a vehicle.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

COAST addresses this event-level gap. Unlike task-level expert selection or decoding multiple full solutions, it decomposes evidence among candidates inside each online decision. The policy-visible state is converted into three complementary scores: local edge-aware compatibility, fleet-relative ownership, and candidate-conditioned lookahead.

\subsection{Dynamic representations and local compatibility}

The GraphEncoder produces distance-aware contextual customer embeddings $H_t^{\mathrm{cust}}=\{\mathbf c_{t,j}\}$; in the policy forward path, it is recomputed only when newly revealed requests change $\mathcal C_t$. At every event, the ActiveVehicleEncoder cross-attends from $\mathbf s_{k_t}^t$ to these embeddings to obtain $\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{act}}$. For each active-vehicle--candidate pair, EdgeFeatureEncoder maps the eight dynamic features---distance, travel time, arrival, waiting, lateness, slack, eligibility, and capacity gap---to $\mathbf e_{t,k_t,j}^{\mathrm{emb}}$, collected in $H_t^{\mathrm{edge}}$.

CrossEdgeFusion forms the local compatibility score by adding an edge-dependent bias to each attention head:
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{att}}
&=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[
\frac{(\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{act}}W_Q^h)(\mathbf c_{t,j}W_K^h)^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right.\\[-2pt]
&\hspace{48pt}\left.+(\mathbf w_b^h)^\top\mathbf e_{t,k_t,j}^{\mathrm{emb}}\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
This score captures the immediate vehicle--customer fit without applying a candidate softmax prematurely.

\subsection{Ownership and anticipatory signals}

COAST maintains a memory tensor $M_t$ with one slot per vehicle. After selecting $j^*$, only the acting slot changes:
\[
\begin{aligned}
M_{t+1}[k_t]&=\tanh\!\left(W_{\mathrm{in}}[\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{act}},\mathbf c_{t,j^*},
\mathbf e_{t,k_t,j^*}^{\mathrm{emb}}]\right.\\[-2pt]
&\hspace{38pt}\left.+W_{\mathrm{hid}}M_t[k_t]\right),\\
M_{t+1}[i]&=M_t[i]\quad(i\ne k_t).
\end{aligned}
\]
The OwnershipHead computes bilinear vehicle--customer logits and turns them into the active vehicle's relative claim:
\[
\begin{aligned}
o_{t,i,j}&=\frac{(W_{\mathrm{veh}}M_t[i])^\top(W_{\mathrm{cust}}\mathbf c_{t,j})}{\sqrt D},\\
S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{owner}}&=
\left.\log\operatorname{softmax}_{i\in\mathcal I}(o_{t,i,j})\right|_{i=k_t}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus ownership is a learned coordination bias across vehicles, not explicit vehicle-to-vehicle message passing. In parallel, the LookaheadHead produces an action-conditioned latent routing score,
\[
S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{look}}=\operatorname{MLP}_{\mathrm{look}}
([\mathbf h_t^{\mathrm{act}},\mathbf c_{t,j},\mathbf e_{t,k_t,j}^{\mathrm{emb}}]),
\]
which is optimized through long-horizon policy returns rather than acting as a calibrated value or $Q$-function.

\subsection{Adaptive fusion and learning}

Let $\mathcal R=\{\mathrm{att},\mathrm{look},\mathrm{owner}\}$ denote the three signals in the left-to-right order of Figure~\ref{fig:coast_overview}. COAST normalizes each $S_{t,j}^{r}$, for $r\in\mathcal R$, only over $j\in\mathcal A_t$, then fuses the resulting three-vector with an MLP:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde S_{t,j}^{r}&=\frac{S_{t,j}^{r}-\mu_t^{r}}
{\sqrt{(\sigma_t^{r})^2+\varepsilon}},\qquad r\in\mathcal R,\\
\mathbf z_{t,j}&=[\widetilde S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{att}},
\widetilde S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{look}},\widetilde S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{owner}}],\\
S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{final}}&=\phi_{\mathrm{fuse}}(\mathbf z_{t,j}).
\end{aligned}
\]
The illustrated vectors are $\mathbf S_t^{\mathrm{att}}=(S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{att}})_{j\in\mathcal A_t}$, $\mathbf S_t^{\mathrm{look}}=(S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{look}})_{j\in\mathcal A_t}$, and $\mathbf S_t^{\mathrm{owner}}=(S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{owner}})_{j\in\mathcal A_t}$. The masked policy is
\[
\pi_\theta(j\mid s_t,k_t)=
\frac{\exp(S_{t,j}^{\mathrm{final}})}
{\sum_{u\in\mathcal A_t}\exp(S_{t,u}^{\mathrm{final}})},\qquad j\in\mathcal A_t.
\]
Training samples from this distribution and optimizes REINFORCE \cite{williams1992simple} with a learned critic baseline, advantage normalization, and entropy regularization; reported inference uses greedy $\arg\max$ decoding. The ownership and lookahead modules have no auxiliary targets and receive learning signal only through the policy objective.
\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

Let $L_c$, $L_v$, and $D$ denote the number of customers, vehicles, and the embedding dimension, respectively. The Transformer-based customer encoder dominates the computational complexity of COAST with $O(L_c^2D)$, identical to existing attention-based routing models. The remaining modules introduce only linear overhead, requiring $O(L_cD)$ for the Active Vehicle Encoder, Edge-aware Compatibility, Candidate-conditioned Lookahead, and Score Fusion, and $O(L_vL_cD)$ for the Ownership Memory. Since $L_v \ll L_c$ in typical DVRPTW instances, these additional components do not change the dominant cost. Consequently, the overall complexity remains $O(L_c^2D)$, preserving the same asymptotic complexity as existing Transformer-based neural routing policies while enabling more expressive routing decisions.

\section{Experimental results} \label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We evaluate the normalized routing objective from Section~\ref{sec:prob}, which combines travel distance, lateness, and unserved-request penalties; lower is better. All neural methods use greedy decoding on fixed, matched test instances. The Solomon-style suite \cite{solomon1987algorithms} contains 176 instances across \texttt{h100}, \texttt{h200}, and \texttt{h400}; PYTH contains 10,240 instances at each scale. COAST and every internal variant use the same instance-generation and optimization protocol, with an ablation changing only its named design. MARDAM \cite{bono2021mardam} is the direct event-driven multi-vehicle comparator; AM \cite{kool2019attention} is the canonical attention-based constructive policy, and PolyNet \cite{hottung2025polynet} is a recent learned-diversity comparator. The GPHH comparison suite \cite{TAM2026114234} additionally provides GPHH and the C+C, C+W, and WIQ+C dispatching baselines.

RQ1 keeps benchmark reporting and statistical inference distinct. On the Solomon-style suite, all eight methods are evaluated on the same 176 instances. We first apply a Friedman omnibus test \cite{friedman1937ranks} across COAST, the three neural baselines, GPHH, and the three dispatching rules, then test the seven prespecified COAST--baseline contrasts with two-sided paired Wilcoxon tests \cite{wilcoxon1945ranking} and Holm correction \cite{holm1979sequential}. A neural-only Friedman test provides a like-for-like complement. These tests quantify consistency across matched test instances, not robustness across independently trained seeds.

\subsection{RQ2: How do dynamism and time windows change the advantage?}

\paragraph{Question.} Does COAST retain an advantage as customer arrivals become more dynamic and time windows become tighter?

We evaluate a full-factorial \texttt{n50m3} grid with four degrees of dynamism (DoD), four time-window (TW) ratios, and 32 matched instances per cell (512 total). This is a \emph{within-support sensitivity analysis}: it varies factors represented in data construction rather than claiming an out-of-distribution result. Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_components} reports all four neural methods on the complete grid with one shared colour scale, so the cellwise comparison is not restricted to a single baseline.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/fig_rq2_four_heatmaps.pdf}
\caption{Cellwise DoD--TW comparison of all four neural methods arranged horizontally. Each panel displays the mean normalized cost of one method over 32 matched instances per cell; lower is better. All panels share a common colour scale, enabling direct visual comparison across methods. COAST is lowest in every cell, while MARDAM is the closest comparator throughout this grid.}
\label{fig:rq2_components}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_components} shows a consistent ordering across the complete grid: COAST has the lowest cost in all 16 cells, MARDAM is next-lowest throughout, and both AM and PolyNet remain higher. MARDAM is therefore the most stringent cellwise comparator. At DoD $=0.10$, COAST's reduction over MARDAM is 0.5--1.5\% across TW ratios; at DoD $=0.75$, it is 2.3--6.2\%. The largest reduction occurs at DoD $=0.75$, TW $=0.25$, and remains 5.6\% and 5.1\% at TW $=0.50$ and $0.75$, respectively. Across the full grid, COAST's reduction ranges from 5.4--13.0\% versus AM and 4.4--13.9\% versus PolyNet. The high-dynamism pattern is therefore not an artifact of selecting one baseline; it is consistent with the greater cost of early decisions when more requests remain to arrive.

The interaction is not monotonic in both factors. At the widest TW ratio ($1.00$), COAST still leads every comparator, but its high-DoD reduction over MARDAM falls to 2.3\%. Thus, the evidence does not support a claim that lookahead or ownership helps uniformly in every condition. Instead, within this grid, the largest COAST margins occur under high dynamism when time windows are not widest. This pattern is consistent with, but does not itself prove, the proposed separation of current vehicle--customer compatibility from fleet coordination and future impact.

The marginal means reinforce the cellwise result. All methods become costlier as TW increases, yet COAST remains nearly flat from DoD $=0.10$ to $0.50$ (43.64 to 43.61) before rising to 44.45 at $0.75$; MARDAM increases monotonically from 44.07 to 46.68. At TW $=1.00$, COAST reaches 45.90, compared with 46.62 for MARDAM and approximately 50.7 for AM and PolyNet. Relative to MARDAM, COAST therefore has a flatter response to arrival dynamism while retaining an advantage throughout the time-window sweep.

\subsection{Limitations}

%The internal comparisons use one trained seed per configuration, so paired tests establish consistency over test instances rather than training-seed robustness. The lookahead head is optimized only through policy gradient, and its context-dependent stress profile motivates multi-seed validation and stronger training signals. Finally, normalized cost is the primary metric; a route-verified decomposition into distance, late service, and skipped requests is deferred to the artifact.
The candidate-conditioned lookahead is trained end-to-end from routing outcomes rather than requiring explicit future-cost labels. While this keeps the signal aligned with the final routing objective, its incremental benefit varies across operating regimes. Future work could investigate lightweight auxiliary supervision or confidence-aware fusion to make its contribution more consistent under different arrival and time-window conditions.

This paper presented \textbf{COAST}, a reinforcement learning framework for the Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (DVRPTW). COAST reformulates customer selection as a decision decomposition problem through three complementary signals: Edge-aware Compatibility for immediate routing compatibility, Ownership Memory for implicit fleet coordination, and Candidate-conditioned Lookahead for anticipatory decision making. Experiments demonstrated consistent improvements over classical and neural baselines, strong cross-scale transfer, and robust performance across controlled dynamic conditions. Ablation results supported the overall effectiveness of the decomposition, although the incremental benefit of lookahead varied across operating regimes.

Future work will investigate lightweight auxiliary supervision and confidence-aware fusion to improve the consistency of lookahead. We also plan to extend COAST to heterogeneous fleets, pickup-and-delivery problems, stochastic travel times, and uncertainty-aware dynamic routing.
